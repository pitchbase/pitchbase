Buyer guide

How to buy small-batch from China without getting scammed

Small orders are where most first-time buyers get hurt, because the amounts feel too small to protect. No checklist can guarantee you will not be scammed; these steps reduce the risk.

PitchBase Fields buyer guide · Last update 2026-10-29

Start with a trial order

Place the smallest order the seller genuinely accepts, and treat it as a test of the whole chain — communication, samples, production, shipping, and after-sales — not just of the product. A seller who performs on a trial has earned a bigger order; a seller who cannot perform on a trial will not perform better at scale.

Confirm everything in writing

Product specifications, MOQ, unit price, packaging, lead time, shipping terms, and what happens with defects — all in one written thread before you pay. Verbal assurances and chat messages scattered across apps are hard to rely on later. If a seller will not confirm basics in writing, that is information.

Payment discipline

Be cautious of full payment upfront to a seller you have not verified, and of pressure to pay quickly or through an unusual channel. Stage payments against agreed milestones where the seller accepts them, and keep records of every payment and what it was for. Confirm the receiving account name matches the company you are dealing with, and pause if payment details change mid-order without a clear, verifiable reason.

Verify who you are paying

Check the seller's public footprint independently — official site, platform storefront, cited source pages — rather than trusting documents the seller sends you. Cross-check the company name, account name, and contact details across at least two independent public sources before a first payment.

Inspect before the balance

For a first order, agree third-party inspection before the balance is paid, with scope, sampling, and report format fixed in advance. Inspection costs money; losing a full shipment costs more. See Inspection for firms that do this work.

Red flags worth pausing on

  • Pressure to move the conversation and payment off the platform where you found the seller, with no verifiable reason.
  • Bank details that change mid-order, or an account name that does not match the company name.
  • No public footprint outside the seller's own claims — no site, no storefront, no cited source you can open.
  • Prices far below every comparable listing, with no explanation of what is different.
  • Refusal of samples, of inspection, or of written specifications.

Any one of these is a reason to pause and check further, not an automatic verdict. Several together are a reason to walk away.
