How to verify a China supplier in 10 steps

A method for checking a supplier from public sources before you pay. Verification reduces risk; it does not remove it, and nothing here promises a successful order.

PitchBase Fields buyer guide · Last update 2026-10-29

Start from the directory entry, then follow its cited source yourself. Each entry on this site lists the public page it came from, so every check below can be done without taking the row on trust.

  1. Match the listing to your need. Category, region, and stated MOQ and price should fit your order before you spend time on the rest. A mismatch here is the cheapest reason to stop.
  2. Open the cited source. Read the public page the entry came from. If the page no longer says what the row says, trust the page and treat the row as stale.
  3. Confirm the company has its own public footprint. An official site, a platform storefront, or public registry mentions that you can find independently. A company with no footprint outside its own claims is a reason to slow down, not a verdict.
  4. Establish factory or trader. Ask for production evidence — workshop photos, production process, equipment — and compare it with what the listing states. The listing states a factory-or-trader determination only where the cited page supports it.
  5. Confirm specifications in writing. Materials, dimensions, tolerances, and packaging, agreed before production. Approve a sample against those specifications before a bulk order.
  6. Confirm MOQ, price, and what is included. Unit price, tooling, packaging, and shipping are separate lines until the seller says otherwise in writing. Confirm which of them the quoted price covers.
  7. Check certifications by scope, not by logo. A mark on a page is not proof of scope. Confirm the standard, the product range covered, and validity, with the issuer or lab where that is publicly checkable.
  8. Check export evidence. Stated destinations or export activity are a starting point. Ask for references you can check independently rather than screenshots alone.
  9. Use third-party inspection for a first order. Agree scope, sampling method, and report format before the goods ship, and agree in advance what happens when findings fail.
  10. Put payment terms in writing. Stage payments against inspection or shipment milestones, and keep every confirmation in one thread. Written terms are what you fall back on if anything goes wrong.

What verification cannot do

Public checks cannot see a supplier's internal finances, cannot guarantee the quality of your specific batch, and cannot force a refund. They narrow the field and surface problems early. Contracts, samples, and inspection terms do the rest.
